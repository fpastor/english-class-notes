# U2.3 Changes in security in the workplace

In this lesson, you will give advantages and disadvantages about security measures, learn how to talk about changes, and decide on the use of finished or unfinished activities to refer to security and safety updates.

## Key words of the lesson

|Past actions with a specific time|Past actions with an effect in the present|
|---|---|
|Specific time reference|Non-specific time reference|
|I spoke to her yesterday.|I have spoken to her before.|
|I went to India a year ago.|I've been to India twice.|
|They launched the product in 2016.|They've just launched the product.|
|yesterday - a year ago - in 2016|just - already - yet - still - recently|

## Changes

**Look at the following sentences.**

1. We've decided to upgrade the system. 
2. We've been having a few problems in the present.  
  
In both sentences we are showing a change. We use **perfect tenses** to do that.  
  
We use the **Present Perfect Tense** to talk about things in which there is a connection between the past and the present.  
  
1. **We've worked on the project for a month.** = We started last month. The action is finished.  

We can use the **Present Perfect Continuous** Tense to talk about events with a connection to the present.

2. **We've been working on the project for a month.** = We started last month but we're still working on the project. The action is unfinished.

## Present Perfect Tense (1/4): Form

**FORM:** have / has + past participle  
  
**USAGE:** It expresses an action that is still going on or that stopped recently but has an influence on the present. It puts emphasis on the result.  
  
- *I've discovered a security breach.*
- *He's decided to change his password.*

## Present Perfect Tense (2/4): Present result of the past

The present perfect often links a present situation with something that happened at an unspecified time in the past. Therefore, we do not use specific time expressions such as yesterday, last week, in 1998, two days ago, etc.  
  
*I have given your report to the M.D.*
(Past action: I gave her your report yesterday. Present result: She has the report now.)  
  
We use other types of time expressions such as: just / recently / before / yet / still. All these expressions so not mark a specific point of time reference. They are undefined time references.  
  
*I've been here before.*

## Present Perfect Tense (3/4): Specific and non-specific time

If we say **when** something happened, we use the past simple, not the present perfect:  
  
*I have spoken to her yesterday.* ❌ **INCORRECT**
*I spoke to her yesterday.* ✔️ **CORRECT**  
  
Similarly, with expressions such as on *Monday, in 1987, at 3:30, etc.*, or with questions beginning *When...?* and *How long ago...?*, we use the Past Simple, not the Present Perfect Tense.

## Present Perfect Tense (4/4): Using Just

The Present Perfect Tense is often used with the word **just** to talk about very recent news or actions that have taken place very recently. Again, the exact time is not mentioned:  

*I'm sorry, Mrs. Sammuels is not here. She has **just** left.*

However, in American English you can also say: **She just left.**

## Been or Gone? (1/2)

Notice the difference between **has been** and **has gone**:  
  
*I'm afraid Mr. Jones is not here at the moment. He **has gone** to a meeting in London.*
(He is still at the meeting.)  
  
*Alice **has been** to the travel agent. She has your tickets for Dubai.*
(She has the tickets and has left the travel agent.)

## Present Perfect Continuous Tense(1/3): Form

**FORM:** have / has + been + verb + ing  
  
**USAGE:** It expresses an action that recently stopped or is still going on. It puts emphasis on the duration or course of the action.  
  
**TIME EXPRESSIONS USED:** all day, for (four) years, since (1993), how long….?, the whole (week).  
  
- *They've been discussing safety at the workplace all day.* 
- *She's been trying to find a solution to the security breakdown.*

## Present Perfect Continuous Tense (2/3): Ongoing activities

Use the **Present Perfect Continuous Tense** with *for, since, How long...?* and other expressions of duration (e.g.,all day), to talk about activities starting in the past and still happening now. The activity may have been going on continuously or repeated several times.  
  
- *They have been producing screens here for ten years.* (They started producing screens ten years ago. They are still producing them.)  
- *I have been living in Colombia for the last three years.* (I started living there three years ago, and I still live there.)  
  
However, we normally use the **Present Perfect Tense** with stative verbs (like / believe / smell / love / hate / seem) or for situations we consider permanent.  

- *Karen has been in Dallas since 9 o'clock this morning.* (❌ not: has been being...)
- *I have lived in Missouri all my life.* (❌ not: have been living...)

## Present Perfect Continuous Tense (3/3): Negatives

In the negative, the **present perfect tense** focuses on the amount of time that has passed since something happened. The **present perfect continuous** focuses on the verb itself.  
  
Present Perfect: *I haven't had a vacation for five years.* (The last time was five years ago.)  

Present Perfect Continuous: *I haven't been receiving many phone calls recently.* (This has been continuing for days.)
